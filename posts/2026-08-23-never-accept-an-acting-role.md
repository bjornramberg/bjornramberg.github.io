Your manager resigns on a Tuesday. By Friday, you're in a meeting where HR or your manager's manager asks if you can "step in temporarily" until they find a replacement. You say yes, it feels like the right thing to do, and maybe like the chance you've been waiting for. Nobody mentions an end date. Nobody mentions what happens to your own workload in the meantime.

In a nutshell, an acting role is about holding the fort. The role is the bridge between what was and what will be.

### The Promise

On paper, it sounds like a win-win. You bail out your manager and the wider team, and in return you get a real shot at a bigger scope, maybe a preview of what the next level actually involves and a chance to show upper management what you can do under pressure.

### The Reality (and the Downsides)

Here's where reality hits. Acting roles are notoriously sticky. It's easy to take one on with no end date attached. Eight months later you are still in it — no pay adjustment, no conversation about making it permanent, just the assumption that "we keep going" because things were working fine. If the company hits a budget freeze or a hiring pause, your temporary fix quietly becomes the cheapest solution on the table.

The most dangerous part? You're usually expected to carry 100% of the acting role's responsibilities, even though you realistically only have 30-50% of your time available since you're still holding on to parts of your old job. Do that for six months and you're not "stepping up" anymore, you're just doing two jobs badly instead of one job well.

### So, are acting roles bad?

It's not. The problem isn't the role itself, it's the expectation mismatch. Get the conditions right, and an acting role can genuinely accelerate your career. But getting the conditions right means you have to negotiate them up front, not hope they sort themselves out.

Here are 6 tips to navigate an acting role successfully:

**1. Define the Scope (You Can't Do It All)**
Before you say yes, sit down with the manager responsible and get specific about what the role actually covers. Say you have 30-50% of your time available — what does that buy you? Keeping the team's day-to-day running? Owning people management? Making the technical calls? Realistically, pick one, maybe two. If nobody names what gets dropped, everything you don't get to becomes a mark against you later, even the things you were never given time for.

**2. Set Up Dedicated Check-Ins**
Don't let the acting role live inside your regular 1:1s, where it'll always lose to whatever's more urgent that week. Put a separate recurring meeting on the calendar, just for this. Use it to talk about how the dual workload is actually going, what's slipping, and what support you need. Treat it like its own project with its own status updates, not a side note in a meeting about something else.

**3. Clarify What's In It For You**
You just solved a real problem for the team and your manager. What's the return? Have a direct conversation about what happens if you deliver. Put it in concrete terms: "If I hit these two goals over the next six months, we agree this converts into a permanent promotion." Vague goodwill ("we'll see how it goes") isn't a commitment, get something you can point back to later.

**4. Set a Hard Expiration Date**
"Temporary" needs an actual date on it, three months out, for example. When that date hits, the acting arrangement formally ends: either the organization comes back to renegotiate, you've hit your goals and a promotion conversation starts, or you hand the responsibilities back and return to your original scope. Without a date, "temporary" just means "until someone remembers to change it," which can be never.

**5. Discuss Compensation**
An acting role almost always comes with more responsibility: people, systems, budget, or product decisions that weren't yours before. Ask for a salary bump or an acting allowance for the length of the assignment. Three months of extra responsibility should mean three months of extra pay. If you genuinely see it as a learning opportunity and you're fine with the other terms, you can choose to waive this. But that should be a choice you make, not a gap nobody brings up.

**6. Communicate Your Mandate to the Team**
An acting manager without clear authority is just a scapegoat. Make sure your manager tells the team and stakeholders, explicitly, what your mandate actually is. Don't be a placeholder that keeps the seat warm. You won't be able to make real decisions, and you'll take the blame for problems you had no authority to fix.

Of course, all of this assumes the acting role comes as an addition to your existing obligations and responsibilities. If it's instead a full temporary move to a new position, where someone else takes over your regular job while you act in the new one, that's a different situation. The checklist above still applies generally, but the time-split problem in tip 1 mostly goes away.
