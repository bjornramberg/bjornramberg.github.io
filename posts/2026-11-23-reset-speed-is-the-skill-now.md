---
title: Reset speed is the skill now
date: 2026-11-23
tags: [leadership, learning, technology]
summary: You can't learn it all, and it's only getting harder. The advantage goes to whoever can reset fastest.
---

Things are moving too fast to learn it all. That used to be the job: pick a stack, go deep, become the person or team who knows. Now the half-life of what you know is measured in months. New model, new tool, new pattern, new best practice that contradicts last quarter's best practice. By the time you've "learned it," it's already shifted underneath you.

The power isn't in learning it all, and it isn't in coverage. It's in reset speed.

How fast can you drop a mental model that stopped working and pick up a better one? How fast can you go from "this is how we do it" to "that was then, what works now?" Teams and individuals who do that well are pulling away, not because they know more, but because they restart faster.

I've watched this up close for 25 years in infra. The engineers who survived every wave (virtualization, cloud, containers, Kubernetes, now AI-assisted everything) weren't the ones with the biggest brains. Never was. They were the ones with the shortest grief cycle for their old expertise. They'd shrug, archive the runbook, and learn the new thing without making it an identity crisis. This is not blind happy-go-lucky, this is the ability to challenge and examine and allow your verdict to be based on something you know is temporary.

Resetting fast has two parts, and most people only practice one.

The first is to unlearn and relearn on purpose — and to do it small. Unlearning isn't forgetting, it's demoting. That Terraform pattern you love, that leadership habit that worked with a team of five, that strong opinion about which cloud or language is "right" — move it from law to hypothesis. So the question to you becomes: if I joined this team today with no history, would I still choose this? If the answer is no, you have your reset target.

Make it small and fast. Don't rewrite the world, run a one-week spike. Write the script in the language you swore you'd never touch. Let a junior show you their AI workflow instead of defending yours. Speed comes from reps, not from big-bang rewrites.

The second part is the one that keeps fast reset from turning into thrashing: know where your biases live.

Everyone has them. Vendor comfort. Language loyalty. Architecture scars from the last outage. A leadership style that worked once and became personality. If you don't know yours, you'll "reset" straight into the same shape with new branding. Kubernetes all over again, just with agents.

So inventory them. Literally. What do you default to under pressure? What do you dismiss without trying? What advice do you give every time regardless of context? That's your bias map. Mine includes a reflex toward proven infra based on how complex I see the challenge to be. Useful 80% of the time, blinding the other 20%. Knowing that keeps me honest with myself in asking: is this my judgment talking, or my history?

Put the two together and you get something rare: someone who can change their mind quickly without losing their footing. Fast unlearning gives you motion. Bias awareness gives you steering.

A practical way to start:

**1. Put an expiry date on decisions.** "We do it this way until March, then we revisit." It makes reset expected, whether it was a good or bad call. Works with technical, architectural and leadership decisions.

**2. Name the bias out loud.** In the design doc, in the retro: "My bias here is toward self-hosting because I've been burned by SaaS pricing. Push back if I'm over-rotating." It gives the team permission to correct you faster (and stops AI from just confirming your bias based on your own push).

You won't out-learn the churn. Nobody will. And it will likely even burn you out trying. But you can out-reset it.

The powerhouse isn't the person who knows it all. It's the person who can shed what isn't working and start clean — and knows exactly what baggage they're bringing into the next round.
